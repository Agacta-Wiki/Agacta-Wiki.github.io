# Parliamentary Elections in Agacta

## Overview

Parliamentary elections in Agacta are held every four years to elect the 240 members of the Parliament of Agacta. Elections have followed a regular four-year cycle since the introduction of the Constitution of 1952, with the first parliamentary election under the modern constitutional system taking place in 1953.

Agacta operates a mixed-member proportional (MMP) electoral system, combining directly elected representatives from single-member sub-constituencies with compensatory party-list representation.

The system is designed to balance proportional representation with a strong emphasis on directly elected parliamentary mandates. Unlike some other MMP systems, Agacta does not create additional parliamentary seats to compensate for disproportional results. Instead, the proportional allocation is adjusted to accommodate directly elected MPs while maintaining the fixed size of Parliament.

Agacta's parliamentary politics has historically been dominated by the centre-left Socialist Party and centre-right Constitution Party, with the centrist Liberal Party frequently holding the balance of power. The Green Party and Agacta Left Bloc have also become significant parliamentary forces.

The most recent parliamentary election was held in 2025, resulting in a fourth consecutive Constitution–Liberal coalition government. The next scheduled parliamentary election is in 2029.

---

## Parliamentary Structure

The Parliament of Agacta is a unicameral legislature consisting of 240 Members of Parliament (MPs).

Members serve four-year terms and are elected from 19 geographical constituencies corresponding to Agacta's counties.

The four overseas autonomous territories, Aquatrip, Savtrio Islands, Tlelmund and Ulos, are each treated as one county for national parliamentary electoral purposes.

Parliamentary seats are allocated among the 19 counties according to their populations.

Each county's parliamentary representation is divided between directly elected MPs and MPs elected through compensatory county party lists.

The allocation of seats to counties determines the number of MPs each county elects, but does not establish separate classes of parliamentary membership. All MPs have equal parliamentary status, regardless of their method of election.

# Electoral System

## Mixed-Member Proportional Representation

Agacta uses a county-based mixed-member proportional electoral system.

Each county's parliamentary seats are divided equally between two methods of election:

1. **Direct representation:** Half of the county's MPs are elected from single-member sub-constituencies using first-past-the-post voting.
2. **Party-list representation:** The remaining seats are initially allocated through a compensatory system using countywide party votes and the D'Hondt method.

The two components are connected rather than operating as separate elections.

The proportional calculation determines the total number of parliamentary seats to which each qualifying party is entitled within the county. Directly elected MPs count towards their party's entitlement, with the remaining seats filled from the party list.

Where a party wins more direct seats than its proportional entitlement, the direct mandates are protected and the proportional calculation is adjusted.

The total number of parliamentary seats remains fixed at 240.

### Directly Elected MPs

Each county is divided into single-member sub-constituencies.

Voters elect one representative in each sub-constituency using first-past-the-post voting.

The candidate receiving the greatest number of votes is elected, regardless of whether they receive an absolute majority.

These directly elected MPs are guaranteed their parliamentary seats.

A successful direct candidate cannot be deprived of their mandate because their party has won more direct seats than its proportional entitlement.

This principle is fundamental to Agacta's electoral system.

### Compensatory Party Lists

The remaining parliamentary seats are allocated through countywide party lists.

Parties receive seats according to their share of the qualifying party-list vote, calculated using the D'Hondt method.

An electoral threshold of 5% applies to party-list representation.

Because Agacta's system is compensatory, the number of directly elected MPs belonging to each party is taken into account when determining how many additional MPs that party receives from its list.

A party which wins substantial direct representation may therefore receive relatively few additional list seats.

A party which receives significant countywide support but wins few or no sub-constituencies may receive most or all of its parliamentary representation through the list.

---

## The D'Hondt Calculation

The D'Hondt method is used to determine the proportional entitlement of qualifying political parties within each county.

Under D'Hondt, each party's vote total is divided by a series of divisors beginning with 1, 2, 3, 4 and continuing as necessary.

The available seats are assigned to the highest resulting quotients.

For example, a party receiving 100,000 votes produces the following initial quotients:

| Divisor | Quotient |
|---|---:|
| 1 | 100,000 |
| 2 | 50,000 |
| 3 | 33,333.33 |
| 4 | 25,000 |
| 5 | 20,000 |

The quotients of all qualifying parties are compared to determine their proportional seat entitlements.

However, Agacta applies an additional procedure to protect directly elected parliamentary mandates.

This distinguishes its system from MMP arrangements which permit overhang seats or expand the legislature through balancing seats.

---

## Protection of Directly Elected Mandates

### Constituency Primacy

Agacta's electoral system is built around the principle that directly elected representatives must retain their seats.

Where a party wins more sub-constituencies than its proportional entitlement, its successful direct candidates remain elected.

Rather than increasing the size of Parliament, the proportional calculation is adjusted to accommodate these additional mandates.

This procedure is sometimes necessary when a party wins a particularly large number of sub-constituencies despite receiving a smaller proportion of the countywide party-list vote.

### The Adjustment Procedure

The adjustment operates as follows:

1. The county's full parliamentary seat allocation is initially distributed proportionally using D'Hondt.
2. Each party's proportional entitlement is compared with the number of sub-constituencies it has won.
3. If a party has won more direct seats than its proportional entitlement, those excess direct mandates are protected.
4. The total number of seats used in the D'Hondt calculation is reduced.
5. D'Hondt is recalculated using the reduced number of proportional seats.
6. The calculation continues until the proportional allocation, together with any additional protected direct mandates, equals the county's prescribed parliamentary seat allocation.

The procedure also accommodates situations in which more than one party simultaneously holds excess direct mandates.

At no stage are successful directly elected MPs removed from Parliament to restore proportionality.

The county's total parliamentary representation does not increase.

### Example: A Twelve-Seat County

Consider a county entitled to 12 parliamentary seats.

Six MPs are elected directly, while the remaining seats are initially available through the compensatory list system.

The countywide party-list votes are:

| Party | Votes |
|---|---:|
| Socialist Party | 112,000 |
| Constitution Party | 115,000 |
| Liberal Party | 80,000 |
| Green Party | 53,000 |
| Agacta Left Bloc | 22,000 |

Suppose the Socialist Party wins all six directly elected seats.

This creates a potential conflict between the party's direct representation and its proportional entitlement.

The adjustment procedure reduces the number of seats used for the D'Hondt distribution until all six Socialist MPs can be accommodated without increasing the county's total representation.

Using a nine-seat D'Hondt calculation gives the following distribution:

| Party | D'Hondt Seats | Additional Protected Direct Seats | Final Seats |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Socialist Party | 3 | 3 | 6 |
| Constitution Party | 3 | 0 | 3 |
| Liberal Party | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Green Party | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Agacta Left Bloc | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total** | **9** | **3** | **12** |

The Socialist Party therefore retains all six directly elected MPs.

The Constitution Party receives three list MPs, the Liberal Party receives two, and the Green Party receives one.

The county still elects exactly 12 MPs.

Although the final distribution is less proportional than an ordinary 12-seat D'Hondt allocation, this is permitted because direct mandates take precedence.

### Example: Multiple Parties with Excess Direct Seats

The adjustment mechanism can also operate where more than one party wins more direct seats than its proportional entitlement.

Consider a county entitled to 20 MPs, with ten elected directly.

Suppose the Constitution Party wins seven sub-constituencies and the Green Party wins three.

After reducing the D'Hondt allocation to 17 seats, the proportional calculation produces the following distribution:

| Party | D'Hondt Seats | Additional Protected Direct Seats | Final Seats |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Socialist Party | 6 | 0 | 6 |
| Constitution Party | 5 | 2 | 7 |
| Liberal Party | 3 | 0 | 3 |
| Green Party | 2 | 1 | 3 |
| Agacta Left Bloc | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| **Total** | **17** | **3** | **20** |

Both the Constitution Party and Green Party retain all their directly elected MPs.

The adjustment protects two additional Constitution mandates and one additional Green mandate.

The final distribution contains 20 MPs, with no additional seats created.

### Disproportionality

The system accepts that protecting directly elected MPs can produce outcomes which are less proportional than the original D'Hondt calculation.

This is an intentional characteristic of Agactan electoral law.

Unlike some mixed-member systems, Agacta does not use balancing seats to compensate other political parties for the effects of excess direct mandates.

The priorities of the system are:

1. Protecting direct parliamentary mandates.
2. Maintaining a fixed Parliament of 240 members.
3. Achieving proportional representation as closely as the first two requirements permit.

# Party Lists and Candidate Selection

## Semi-Open Party Lists

Agacta currently operates a semi-open party-list system.

Political parties establish an initial ordering of their candidates, but voters may influence which candidates ultimately receive the party's allocated parliamentary seats.

Voters may cast their party-list vote for a political party as a whole or express a preference for an individual candidate appearing on that party's county list.

A personal vote for a candidate also counts towards the total vote received by that candidate's party.

Consequently, personal voting preferences influence the selection of individual MPs without changing the number of votes attributed to the party for proportional representation.

### The Twelve-Percent Rule


A candidate receiving more than 12% of their party's total countywide vote may move ahead of candidates placed above them in the original party-list ordering.

Where multiple candidates exceed the 12% threshold, they are ranked according to the number of personal votes received, from highest to lowest.

Candidates who do not reach this threshold remain subject to the party's established list order.

For example, suppose the Liberal Party receives 100,000 countywide votes.

A candidate placed sixth on its list receives 15,000 personal votes.

That candidate has received 15% of the party's total vote and therefore exceeds the 12% threshold.

The candidate can move ahead of other candidates in the party's original list ordering, potentially securing a parliamentary seat ahead of someone originally ranked higher.

If several candidates exceed the threshold, the candidate receiving the most personal votes is placed highest among them, followed by the candidate receiving the second-highest number, and so on.

The rule allows voters to exert a meaningful influence over candidate selection while retaining an established party list as the starting point.

It is distinct from a fully open-list system in which candidates are simply ranked according to their personal vote totals.

The 12% candidate-preference threshold is separate from the 5% electoral threshold used for proportional representation.




## Candidates Contesting Both Components

Candidates may simultaneously contest an individual sub-constituency and appear on their party's county list.

Losing a direct election does not necessarily prevent a candidate from entering Parliament.

A candidate who fails to win their sub-constituency may still be elected through the party-list allocation.

This arrangement enables parties to place prominent candidates on their lists while also allowing them to contest direct elections.

However, list placement does not guarantee election.

Where a party's entire parliamentary entitlement is occupied by directly elected MPs, it receives no additional list seats.

A prominent politician can therefore lose their direct election and fail to enter Parliament despite occupying a high position on their party's list.

Such unexpected defeats are sometimes compared to the political phenomenon known as a "Portillo moment".

## Party Primaries

Political parties use internal selection procedures to determine their initial candidate-list orderings.

Since 2005, the Single Transferable Vote (STV) has been the standard method used for internal party primaries.

STV allows party members to rank candidates in order of preference.

The resulting selection process establishes the initial ordering of candidates before the parliamentary election.

Political parties are also required to provide accessible written explanations of their candidate-list ordering.

Although parties are not prohibited from placing prominent or popular candidates unusually low on their lists, such decisions may attract political criticism.

Voters retain the opportunity to elevate individual candidates through the semi-open list system.

# Historical Development of Party Lists

## Closed Party Lists

Before 1989, Agacta used closed party lists.

Under this system, political parties established the order in which their candidates would receive parliamentary seats.

Voters could support a party list but could not directly alter the ordering of its candidates.

The selection of list MPs was therefore largely controlled by party organisations.

## Introduction of Open Lists in 1989

Fully open party lists were introduced for the 1989 parliamentary election.

The new system allowed voters greater influence over which candidates received parliamentary seats.

It was also used in the 1993 election.

However, the reforms produced numerous unexpected outcomes.

Prominent and established politicians sometimes lost parliamentary seats after voters elevated candidates who had originally been placed lower on their party lists.

The number of unexpected defeats became a subject of political controversy.

## Reforms Beginning in 1997

The 1997 parliamentary election marked the introduction of Agacta's semi-open party-list system, replacing the fully open lists used in 1989 and 1993. Under the new system, parties retained an initial ordering of candidates, while voters could elevate candidates who received more than 12% of their party's countywide vote.

Following the experiences of the 1989 and 1993 elections, Agacta introduced party primaries in 1997 to give party members a greater role in establishing candidate lists.

Initially, political parties were permitted to choose their own methods for conducting primaries.

This produced differences in candidate-selection procedures between parties.

The reforms formed part of the development of the current electoral framework, which seeks to balance party selection procedures with voter influence.

## Standardisation of STV in 2005

In 2005, STV became the standard method for internal party primaries.

Party members could rank candidates in order of preference, providing a structured means of determining their party's initial candidate-list ordering.

The modern semi-open party-list system combines these internal selection procedures with the ability of general-election voters to elevate candidates who receive substantial individual support.

# Independent Candidates

Independent candidates are permitted to participate in parliamentary elections.

They may contest individual sub-constituencies using first-past-the-post voting.

Independents may also establish their own electoral lists for the proportional component.

An independent candidate may submit a list containing only their own name.

However, independent parliamentary representation is relatively uncommon.

Candidates standing without an established political party generally face substantial obstacles under both components of the system.

In direct elections, they must receive more votes than every other candidate in their sub-constituency.

Under the proportional component, they must obtain sufficient countywide support to qualify for representation.

The 5% electoral threshold can create an additional difficulty for independent lists.

# Parliamentary Representation

## County-Wide Mandates

All Agactan MPs represent their entire county, regardless of whether they were elected directly or through the party-list system.

This is an important distinction between Agacta and electoral systems in which directly elected representatives are formally responsible only for their individual constituencies.

For example, an MP elected from Treslio Sub-Constituency 5 is a representative of Treslio County as a whole.

The sub-constituency establishes the geographical area in which that MP was directly elected, but it does not restrict the MP's parliamentary mandate to that area.

## Constituency Responsibilities

Political parties generally divide constituency-service responsibilities among their elected MPs.

Directly elected MPs retain responsibility for the sub-constituencies in which they won election.

Other areas of the county are allocated among the party's remaining MPs, including list-elected representatives.

These arrangements usually follow geographical boundaries where practical.

For example, a party which wins two direct seats in a county but receives four MPs overall may assign the two list MPs responsibility for other areas where it did not win a direct election.

This helps provide voters with access to parliamentary representatives from their preferred political party even when that party loses their immediate sub-constituency.

The arrangements also ensure that all parts of a county have access to directly elected parliamentary representation through the sub-constituency system.

---

# Parliamentary Vacancies

Different procedures apply depending on how an MP was originally elected.

## Directly Elected MPs

When a directly elected MP leaves Parliament, the vacancy is filled through a by-election in the relevant sub-constituency.

The by-election allows voters in that sub-constituency to choose a replacement representative.

## List-Elected MPs

When a list-elected MP leaves Parliament, the vacancy is filled by the next eligible candidate on the relevant party list.

A new countywide election is not required.

This distinction preserves the principle of direct electoral mandates while maintaining continuity in party-list representation.

# Parliamentary Elections and Government Formation

## Formation of Governments

Following a parliamentary election, the President of Agacta invites the person considered most likely to form a viable government to do so.

This is not necessarily the leader of the party which won the greatest number of parliamentary seats.

Agacta has a longstanding tradition of coalition government.

The Constitution Party and Socialist Party have historically been the two largest parliamentary forces, with the Liberal Party frequently holding the balance of power.

The Liberal Party has participated in governments led by both major parties.

However, the Constitution Party and Socialist Party have never formed a coalition government together.

## Governments Formed by the Second-Largest Party

Agacta has experienced several elections in which the largest individual political party did not form the government.

This occurred in 1969, 1981 and 1993.

On each occasion, the Socialist Party was the largest parliamentary party, but the Constitution Party and Liberal Party formed a coalition government.

These elections demonstrate the importance of parliamentary alliances within Agacta's system.

Winning the largest number of seats does not necessarily guarantee that a party will enter government.

## Minority Governments

Minority governments are constitutionally permitted.

A government may remain in office without holding an outright parliamentary majority, provided it retains sufficient support or tolerance to continue governing.

The parliamentary system therefore allows governments to be formed through coalition agreements, confidence arrangements or other forms of parliamentary cooperation.

## Early Parliamentary Elections

Although the Constitution permits early parliamentary elections, none have occurred since the establishment of the modern republic.

When a government loses the confidence of Parliament, the established political practice is to seek an alternative administration within the existing legislature.

The President plays an important role in facilitating negotiations between political parties.

Consequently, Agacta has maintained an uninterrupted four-year parliamentary electoral cycle since 1953.

# Political History

## Socialist Dominance, 1953–1969

The first parliamentary election under the Constitution of 1952 took place in 1953.

The Socialist Party won an outright parliamentary majority and formed the first government under the modern constitutional system.

It retained its majority following the elections of 1957, 1961 and 1965.

These four consecutive victories established the Socialist Party as the dominant political force in the early republic.

The period ended following the 1969 parliamentary election.

Although the Socialists remained the largest individual parliamentary party, the Constitution Party and Liberal Party formed a coalition government.

This demonstrated that the parliamentary system could produce a change of government without a change in the identity of the largest party.

## Coalition Politics, 1969–1997

The 1969 election established the Constitution–Liberal coalition as an alternative to Socialist government.

The Socialists returned to power following the 1973 election.

After the 1977 election, the Socialist Party formed a coalition government with the Liberals.

The Constitution Party and Liberals returned to government following the 1981 election, again despite the Socialists remaining the largest individual party.

The 1985 election resulted in another Socialist–Liberal coalition.

The Socialists subsequently won an outright parliamentary majority in 1989.

The 1993 election produced another Constitution–Liberal government despite the Socialist Party finishing as the largest parliamentary party.

Throughout this period, the Liberals frequently determined which of the two larger parties could form a governing majority.

Their repeated participation in administrations led by either party established their reputation as Agacta's principal coalition kingmaker.

## Socialist Government, 1997–2013

The Socialist Party returned to government following the 1997 parliamentary election.

It won outright majorities in 1997, 2001 and 2005, establishing a sixteen-year period of Socialist-led government.

The 2001 election took place during a period of strong economic performance and produced one of the party's strongest electoral victories.

The Liberals, facing the prospect of another Socialist majority, emphasised the importance of providing a parliamentary counterweight to the governing party.

The 2009 election brought an end to the Socialists' consecutive outright majorities.

The party nevertheless remained in government by forming a coalition with the Green Party.

The election also marked the parliamentary arrival of the Agacta Left Bloc, which had been founded earlier that year.

The Socialist-led period ended following the 2013 general election.

## Constitution-Led Government, 2013–Present

The 2013 parliamentary election marked a significant political change.

For the first time, the Constitution Party became the largest individual parliamentary party.

It formed a coalition government with the Liberal Party.

The coalition remained in office following the 2017 and 2021 elections.

The 2021 election also produced a significant change in the parliamentary opposition.

The Green Party overtook the Socialist Party and Liberals to become the Official Opposition.

However, the Greens subsequently faced increasing criticism over their opposition performance and growing competition from other political parties.

The 2025 election produced another political realignment.

The Socialist Party recovered strongly and regained its position as the principal parliamentary opposition.

The Greens suffered substantial losses, while the Agacta Left Bloc made significant gains.

The Constitution Party remained the largest party in Parliament and renewed its coalition with the Liberal Party.

The result gave the Constitution–Liberal coalition a fourth consecutive term in government.

# Electoral Philosophy and Debate

Agacta's parliamentary electoral system reflects a compromise between geographical representation and proportional representation.

Its single-member sub-constituencies ensure that voters can elect individual representatives directly.

Its compensatory party lists allow political parties to receive parliamentary representation beyond the sub-constituencies they win.

The semi-open list system gives voters an additional means of influencing which candidates enter Parliament.

However, the system does not seek to guarantee complete mathematical proportionality.

The protection of directly elected MPs may produce outcomes in which a party's final seat allocation differs significantly from its countywide vote share.

This is particularly likely where one party wins a large majority of sub-constituencies despite receiving a substantially smaller share of the party-list vote.

Supporters of constituency primacy emphasise the importance of respecting direct electoral mandates.

The competing argument for greater proportionality concerns the extent to which the final composition of Parliament should reflect voters' party preferences.

Agacta's electoral arrangements give priority to direct mandates and the fixed size of Parliament while preserving proportional representation to the greatest extent compatible with those requirements.
